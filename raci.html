How to read this. R does the work (several allowed). A owns the outcome and cannot delegate it (exactly one). C gives input before the decision. I is told afterwards. The small pills link to the roadmap initiative each decision governs. Select the ⓘ button beside an Accountable role to read why it owns the decision.

Why

The CFO is the only executive who sees the competing claims of every phase at once: foundation spend, customer tools and automation capital. Splitting capital across divisions is a core CFO accountability and a Board-reporting duty, so it cannot sit with an initiative owner who benefits from the answer. Finance and the Transformation Office prepare the cases; the CFO signs the split.

An exception to the shared standards is a technology-risk trade-off: a faster launch outside the platform adds integration debt that later initiatives such as Maersk Flow and the Logistics Hub must pay. The CTIO owns enterprise architecture, and since a CTO joined the Executive Board (Logan, 2021) can escalate and overrule at executive level. One accountable role stops each product team granting its own exceptions.

Data-quality and access rules cut across every business unit, so ownership has to sit above any one unit but close to the people who run the data. A Head of Data/AI reporting to the CTIO owns the platform and its quality standards, while General Counsel and the CISO are consulted on privacy and security. This also stops business units that gain from looser access from policing themselves.

When engineers and budget are short, choosing between the Logistics Hub and predictive maintenance with digital twins is a cross-divisional trade-off between customer-facing and operational work. Only the CEO can make that call without favouring one division’s results. The Transformation Office prepares the ranking; the CEO decides and owns the outcome.

Choosing partners such as IBM for TradeLens, Sea Machines for vessel situational awareness or AGV and RFID suppliers creates contractual, security and supplier-concentration risk. The Chief Procurement Officer owns sourcing policy across divisions, so one role applies the same due-diligence standard. The CTIO, General Counsel and CISO are consulted before the choice, not after.

TradeLens depended on other carriers, ports and customs authorities joining, which no internal executive controls, so the person closest to that ecosystem sees adoption signals first and can recommend continue, realign or decommission. Making this role accountable, rather than the CEO, keeps the call tied to platform adoption data. The CEO, CTIO, IBM and General Counsel are consulted before the decision is made.

Terminal automation changes jobs, shifts and port relationships, and APM Terminals owns the terminal operations, the labor relations and the port-authority relationships involved. Its CEO therefore carries the outcome: service levels, safety and workforce impact. Operations is responsible, and the people function, unions and port authorities are consulted first.
